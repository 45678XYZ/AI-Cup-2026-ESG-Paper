\section{Discussion}

The practical contribution of projection is its guarantee rather than a
universal field-score gain: as a retraining-free layer over compatible
field-wise probabilities, it enforces the supplied hierarchy in every arm and
improves exact whole-tuple accuracy throughout the executed external matrix,
without any claim about the underlying encoder.

\subsection{Why the two metrics disagree}
\label{sec:mechanism}
This guarantee is not cost-free in every scoring view, and
Table~\ref{tab:multilingual} makes the trade-off explicit by counting the
correct field predictions projection creates against those it destroys.
The $N\!/\!A$ net is positive and the
substantive-class net negative in all five fixed arms. If an independently
predicted parent is wrong, projection may replace a correct substantive child
with the structurally required \emph{N/A}; when the parent is correct, the same
operation can repair an unsupported child. Field-wise macro-F1 values these
changes class by class, whereas tuple accuracy credits only four-field
agreement. The field-score effect therefore varies across backbones and
corpora. Post-hoc C-wF1 and hF point in the tuple direction, but were chosen
after the primary analysis and are not needed to establish the pre-specified
secondary result.

\paragraph{Why greedy projection beats joint decoding.} Validity alone does not
identify the better structured rule: M4 searches all 17 legal states yet loses
tuple accuracy to greedy M1. The decoder ranks each legal state by the product
of its four field probabilities, which selects the most probable row only if
the fields are independent given the text, the same assumption M0 makes.
Pooled over both protocols and seeds, it revises promise status relative to
projection on 339 of 12{,}000 rows: 225 become correct and 114 wrong, so the
parent field improves, yet whole-row correctness on those rows falls from 114
to 47. The exchange is asymmetric. Repairing a parent rarely rescues a row
whose children remain wrong, whereas breaking a correct parent always loses
the row. Table~\ref{tab:legality-cost} shows the same split across Chinese
backbones: without structural training, joint decoding leaves official wF1
level or higher but tuple accuracy level or lower. A larger legal-state search
is therefore not automatically better, although joint decoding need not fail
on other models or tasks.

\paragraph{Calibration and factorization.} Decision calibration interacts with
the output rule. Conditional biases do not detectably change projection
(M3--M2, $+0.001$ wF1) yet lower 17-state decoding by $0.008$ (M6--M5, not
surviving correction). A plausible cause is that the three pinned \emph{N/A}
biases of Eq.~\eqref{eq:pinned-biases} distort the comparison across legal
states that only the decoder makes. We hold the factorized four-head model
fixed by design: classifier chains or a direct 17-way classifier would change
what the encoder represents rather than the decision rule, and the latter is
ill-posed here because seven of the 17 legal states occur at most 26 times in
the labeled release, two of them never.

\subsection{Reproducibility}
\label{sec:repro}
\begin{sloppypar}
The repository releases row-level predictions and text-free metadata for all
eight paper tables and Figure~1; raw corpora, model weights, and retraining are
excluded.
\end{sloppypar}

\begingroup\emergencystretch=1em
\paragraph{Reproducibility details.} Released configurations pin exact Hugging
Face revisions for every backbone in the Methods. For each seed, reports are
identifier-sorted, shuffled, stable-sorted by decreasing row count (preserving
shuffled ties), then assigned uniformly to one of the two lightest folds, with
fold index breaking equal loads. Korean pages use Poppler's
\hfid{pdftotext -f P -l P -layout} per labelled (URL, page); image-only pages
fall back to \hfid{pdftoppm -r 250} and Tesseract (\hfid{-l kor+eng}). The
release has one label tuple per unique pair and needs no further deduplication.
The 32 external arms consumed 25.4 GPU-hours on one RTX 3090.
\par\endgroup

\subsection{Limitations}
The corpora differ in language, reports, annotation distributions, and primary
backbones, so the replication matrix is not a factorial estimate of a pure
language effect. English and French each have only nine report clusters, and
even Chinese inference resamples just 49. The $32/32$ and $14/32$ summaries
are therefore descriptive directions across executed arms, not a pooled
significance test. AI CUP weights applied to ML-Promise are not an official
ML-Promise metric.

Every contrast uses M0, unconstrained argmax on the same probabilities, to
isolate the decision rule. We do not compare against a published system,
official baseline, or leaderboard and make no participant-ranking claim.

Korean adds an input mismatch: its 500 examples are first-384-token PDF pages,
not released paragraphs, and include no gold \emph{Misleading} example. We do
not redistribute extracted text or source PDFs. Released predictions can be
rescored, but retraining requires rebuilding pages with the recipe in
Section~\ref{sec:repro}.

\begin{sloppypar}
Structural-loss and backbone results remain exploratory: fixed loss weights
and limited checkpoints cannot characterize all objectives or architectures.
The rarest Chinese quality label has two examples and Korean has none, so no
class-level claim is licensed. Each seed jointly changes folds and optimization
randomness, preventing their effects from being separated.
\end{sloppypar}

\section{Conclusion}

Hierarchical projection is not a guaranteed route to higher field-wise
weighted macro-F1. It is instead a retraining-free validity layer that
eliminates invalid tuples by construction and
improves exact whole-tuple accuracy on the Chinese anchor and in all 32
evaluated external arms.
Its field-score effect varies across models and corpora, but its logical guarantee does not.
For audit-oriented tasks with dependent outputs, field-wise performance,
whole-tuple correctness, and structural validity should be reported
together.
