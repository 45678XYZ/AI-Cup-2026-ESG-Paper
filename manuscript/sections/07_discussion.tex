\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Why the two metrics disagree}
\label{sec:mechanism}
Projection's contribution is a guarantee, not a universal field-score gain, and
the guarantee has a cost in some scoring views: Table~\ref{tab:multilingual}
counts the correct field predictions projection creates and destroys, and the
$N\!/\!A$ net is positive while the substantive-class net is negative in all
five fixed arms. If an independently
predicted parent is wrong, projection may replace a correct substantive child
with the structurally required \emph{N/A}; when the parent is correct, the same
operation can repair an unsupported child. Field-wise macro-F1 values these
changes class by class, whereas tuple accuracy credits only four-field
agreement. The field-score effect therefore varies across backbones and
corpora.

\paragraph{Why greedy projection beats joint decoding.} M4 searches all 17
legal states yet loses tuple accuracy to M1. It ranks states by the product of
field probabilities, which finds the most probable row only if the fields are
independent given the text, the assumption M0 makes. Pooled over protocols and
seeds, it revises promise status relative to projection on 339 of 12{,}000
rows: 225 become correct and 114 wrong, yet whole-row correctness on those rows
falls from 114 to 47. Repairing a parent rarely rescues a row whose children
remain wrong, whereas breaking a correct parent always loses it.
Table~\ref{tab:legality-cost} shows the same split: without structural
training, joint decoding leaves wF1 level or higher but tuple accuracy level or
lower. A wider legal-state search is thus not automatically better, though it
need not fail on other models or tasks.

\paragraph{Calibration and factorization.} Calibration interacts with the
output rule: conditional biases do not detectably change projection (M3--M2,
$+0.001$ wF1) but lower 17-state decoding by $0.008$ (M6--M5, not surviving
correction), plausibly because the pinned \emph{N/A} biases of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:pinned-biases} distort the cross-state comparison only the
decoder makes. We hold the factorized four-head model fixed by design:
classifier chains or a 17-way classifier would change the encoder rather than
the decision rule, and the latter is ill-posed here, as seven of the 17 legal
states occur at most 26 times in the labeled release, two never.

\subsection{Validity in audit use}
\label{sec:audit}
Validity is a precondition for using a prediction as an audit record, not
evidence that the record is right. A contradictory tuple, such as an evidence
quality on a paragraph judged to have no evidence, cannot be filed or counted
without deciding which field to believe; projection decides deterministically,
making every output admissible but not correct. A repair can turn a visible
inconsistency into a consistent error, removing the cue that would have drawn
a reviewer's attention, so a deployed validity layer should log which rows it
repaired.

\paragraph{Choosing between the metrics.} The metrics disagree for two
separable reasons: wF1 credits each correct field even in a wrong or
contradictory row and weights rare classes like common ones, whereas tuple
accuracy credits only whole rows, weighted by frequency. The choice should
follow how outputs are used. (i) If each paragraph becomes one record, such as
an assurance file or a database entry, one wrong field spoils it; compare
rules by tuple accuracy among valid outputs, which here favors projection over
joint decoding. (ii) If one field drives an action, such as routing promises to
analysts, use that field's own F1, and for a rare target such as misleading
evidence its per-class recall. (iii) If outputs feed portfolio statistics,
such as the share of promises with clear evidence, use class-level field
scores but keep validity, since invalid tuples make such counts inconsistent.
(iv) Use official wF1 only where it is the agreed rule, as on a leaderboard,
and even there not to separate rules closer than this benchmark's resolution
(0.008).

\subsection{Reproducibility}
\label{sec:repro}
\begingroup\emergencystretch=1em
The repository releases row-level predictions and text-free metadata for all
eight paper tables and Figure~1; raw corpora, model weights, and retraining are
excluded. Configurations pin each backbone's Hugging Face revision. Per seed, reports are identifier-sorted, shuffled,
stable-sorted by decreasing size, and each assigned to one of the two lightest
folds. Korean pages come from Poppler's \hfid{pdftotext}, with Tesseract for
image-only pages. The 32 external arms took 25.4 GPU-hours on one RTX 3090.
\par\endgroup

\subsection{Limitations}
The replication matrix is not a factorial estimate of a pure language
effect. English and French each have only nine report clusters, and
even Chinese inference resamples just 49. The $32/32$ and $14/32$ summaries
are therefore descriptive directions across executed arms, not a pooled
significance test. AI CUP weights applied to ML-Promise are not an official
ML-Promise metric. Every contrast uses M0, unconstrained argmax on the same probabilities, to
isolate the decision rule. We do not compare against a published system,
official baseline, or leaderboard and make no participant-ranking claim.

Korean page inputs (Section~\ref{sec:task}) are not redistributed: released predictions
can be rescored, but retraining requires the recipe in
Section~\ref{sec:repro}.

\begin{sloppypar}
Structural-loss and backbone results are exploratory; fixed loss weights and
few checkpoints cannot characterize all objectives or architectures. The
rarest Chinese quality label has two examples and Korean none, so no
class-level claim is made. Each seed changes folds and optimization together,
so their effects cannot be separated.
\end{sloppypar}

\section{Conclusion}

Hierarchical projection is not a guaranteed route to higher field-wise
weighted macro-F1. It is instead a retraining-free validity layer that
eliminates invalid tuples by construction and
improves exact whole-tuple accuracy on the Chinese anchor and in all 32
evaluated external arms.
Its field-score effect varies across models and corpora, but its logical guarantee does not.
For audit-oriented tasks with dependent outputs, structural validity should
be a precondition, and the metric that chooses a rule should match the unit
at which an error has consequences.
